Coming home, into your body.

For when you’re stuck in your head, overthinking, ruminating, or halfway down the dark doom hole, and you want to come back to the land of the living. This is an experiment, not homework. Try things, keep what feels good, ditch what doesn’t.

The Tony Robbins Triad

Change your state, change your life.

Your state is how you feel in this exact moment, and Tony Robbins says three things drive it. Pull any one of these levers and your state shifts. Pull all three and it flips. Everything in this app is one of these levers.

1 · PhysiologyYour body

How you breathe, stand, move and touch. Change your body and your brain follows. That’s the whole 55 ways menu.

2 · FocusYour attention

Whatever you focus on, you feel. Pull your focus out of your head and into your five senses, and you’re back in the room.

3 · LanguageYour words

The words you say to yourself, and the meaning you give things. That’s what your mantra is for.

Try all three in 30 seconds. Stand up, shoulders back, chin up, and take a big physiological sigh (physiology). Find one thing you can see and really look at it (focus). Say your mantra out loud, or “I’m here. I’m home.” (language). Notice what changed.

Quick refresher

Where are you living right now?

Remember those incredible little assholes smacking a spoon on a pot just to see what happens? KIDS. Be more like a kid. What do kids do when they’re bored, or they’ve been thinking for too long? They start fidgeting, wanting to move and playing up. This is why school fucks us up so much. We’re forced to be in our heads for far too long, and we exit school like little zombies.

Associated

In your body

Present. Connected. Feeling your feelings directly. You feel, you think, you react, in real time. Like a kid.

Dissociated

In your head

Detached from what’s happening. Analysing the world instead of experiencing it. Your unconscious mind’s escape hatch: too intense, too much, too soon. “Abort. Into the mind ASAP.”

If you’re in your head, you’re dead.Because you’re not here, in the world, experiencing it.

You’re probably in your head if…

  • You suddenly realise you have no idea what someone just said, and now they’re asking you a question.
  • You feel like you’re watching yourself from the outside, or the world feels a bit not-real.
  • You’re worried about what you look like to other people. (Hello, dance floor. “Fuck, does this look stupid?”)
  • You hyperfocused so hard you forgot to pee, eat or drink water. All mind, no body.
  • You lie in bed at night with a flood of thought downloads coming in.
  • Big chunks of your past feel blank.
None of this means you’re broken. It means your brain did a brilliant job of protecting you. Now we get to teach it, slowly, that it’s safe to come back.
Panic, meltdowns and freeze have their own page. Coming home after an amygdala hijack works differently, so it gets its own teaching, tools and plans.
So what is grounding?

Grounding is getting back in your body.

Exiting the overthinking, rumination, dark doom hole your mind was headed down, and returning to the land of the living. It’s signalling to your brain that your body still exists here, and that it’s safe living in it.

Mindfulness, meditation, breathwork… and I know you just checked out.

“Fuck all of those.” Yep. We’re fed so much bullshit online about them. So put your lens-and-perspective hat on. When the yogi who spent years of his life in silent meditation says you need an hour a day, that’s true in his model of the world. In ours? Two minutes will sometimes do the job. So stop stressing about doing it right or doing it perfect.

The three doors home

Mindfulness: come back through your senses

Mindfulness at its core is noticing your surroundings. You connect back with your body by noticing the world around it: what you can hear, see, smell, taste and touch. It’s why hyperfocus makes you forget you need to pee: all your awareness went to the thing in front of you, and your body got left behind.

How it sounds when I do itI can hear the dog next door licking its bowl clean, the ting of metal on cement. I can see all your beautiful faces. I can smell my husband frying onions. I can taste those onions in my mouth. I can feel the pressure of my shoes on my feet and the little ridges of the mouse wheel under my hand. Back in my body.

Try it right now. Where you are, as you are.

Back in your body. That’s it. That’s the whole thing. Notice how your breath feels now.

Meditation: the conveyor belt

I do meditation differently. You’ve heard you need to clear your mind to be “really” meditating. It’s not about that. It’s working a muscle: practising letting go of your attachment to thoughts, and of having to finish every thought.

A thought comes in on a conveyor belt. You pick it up, go “hmm, that’s an interesting thought”, put it back down, and watch it leave. Then the next one comes along.

Keep a journal next to you. Anything you want to consciously expand on, give yourself time for later. Sometimes just writing a quick note means you don’t feel like you have to finish the thought. By writing it down, you’ve cut the cord.

Meditation is just a pause in your day. Remember the rest-and-digest graph: dipping down and resting your brain from all the overstimulating, high mental energy stuff, so your thoughts can catch up with you. If you lie in bed with thought downloads pouring in, that’s a strong sign you didn’t slow down and let them catch up during the day.

Rests your body

TV, doom scrolling, lying on the couch. Lovely. But your brain is still working overtime.

Rests your brain

A conveyor-belt pause, a walk with no podcast, a cuppa with no phone, staring at the clouds.

Movement: breath, sound, touch, motion

Breathwork. Exercise. Massage. Dancing. Humming. Anything that needs your body to move consciously. These don’t usually happen on autopilot. You have to focus, and the focus is on your body. Even just touch counts: running your hands down your legs, fingertips to fingertips.

They’re all nervous system regulation tools. They build a strong, resilient vagus nerve, they’re backed by neuroscience, and they’re rooted in somatic therapy.

How I use them

Three levels of coming home.

The short ones and the long ones are equally important. Together they bring you out of fight or flight and build the baseline you need to reach homeostasis: balance in your mind and body.

Seconds to 2 minDaily routine

Multiple times a day, every day. You notice you’re in your head, you do one, you’re back.

5 to 15 minA few times a week

A little pocket of brain rest in your day. Your thoughts get to catch up with you.

30 min +A few times a month

Booked in the calendar. These are your vagus nerve builders.

Go gently. Coming back into your body can sometimes bring up feelings your brain was protecting you from. That’s normal, and it’s okay to stop, open your eyes, and pick something softer. If something big comes up, bring it to our calls, or to your therapist if you have one. You don’t have to do it alone.
Fight or flight

Coming home after an amygdala hijack.

Your amygdala is your brain’s smoke alarm. When it thinks you’re in danger, it floods your body with adrenaline and cortisol before your thinking brain even gets a say. That’s an amygdala hijack. Your heart races, your breath goes shallow, and logic leaves the building.

You can’t think your way out of a hijack. You have to body your way out. Fast, strong, physical tools first. The gentle stuff comes after.

Which one is happening?

Fight or flight

Racing heart, can’t breathe, hot, shaky, want to run or explode. Your body has too much energy. Give it somewhere to go: cold, intensity, long exhales.

Freeze

Numb, floaty, foggy, far away, can’t move or think. Your body hit pause. Bring it back online with sensation and small movement.

Meltdown

Everything is too loud, too bright, too much. Your cup overflowed. Turn the input down first, then deep pressure and rhythm.

Why the fast tools work

  • Cold water on your face switches on your dive reflex, which slows your heart rate right down. It’s one of the DBT “TIPP” skills therapists teach for exactly these moments.
  • Sour lollies, ice, strong scent are big sensations that yank your attention out of the panic loop and into your body.
  • Intense movement burns off the fight-or-flight energy your body just pumped out.
  • The physiological sigh (I call it the psychological sigh) is the fastest breathing reset we know of. The long exhale applies the brakes to your nervous system in under a minute.
Watch the physiological sigh Watch my breathing lesson
Skip the cold water face if you have a heart condition, and check with your doctor if you’re unsure. Cold hands or a cold pack on your neck is gentler.
When it’s all too much

Meltdowns aren’t a choice.

A meltdown is what happens when your nervous system hits overload. Too much noise, light, emotion, demand or change, for too long, and the cup overflows. It can look like screaming, crying, snapping, or shutting right down. It’s not a tantrum. It’s not you being dramatic. It’s your body saying: I can’t take in any more.

Watch: my unhinged 4-step nervous system resetFor AuDHD’ers · 5:30 · YouTube
FirstLess input

Lights down, headphones on, somewhere small and quiet, stop talking. Less before more.

ThenPressure and rhythm

Weighted blanket, a tight hug, rocking, humming, butterfly taps. Stimming is regulating.

AfterRecover, zero shame

Water, a snack, rest. Meltdowns are exhausting. You didn’t do anything wrong.
